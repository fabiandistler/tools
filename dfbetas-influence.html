What DFBETAS actually measures

Fit the model once with all n rows. Then delete row i, fit again, and look at how far coefficient bj moved. That raw movement is DFBETA. Divide it by the standard error of the coefficient (estimated without row i) and you get DFBETAS — a unitless number you can compare across predictors and across datasets:

DFBETASj(i) = ( bj − bj(i) ) / ( s(i) · √(X′X)−1jj )

Read it as: “dropping this one row moves this one coefficient by X standard errors.” DFBETAS = 0.8 for income means that row alone shifts the income effect by 0.8 SE — often enough to flip a conclusion. The sign tells you the direction the row is pulling: positive means the coefficient is higher because of that row.

Which column of the row is to blame?

DFBETAS factors exactly into two interpretable pieces (no approximation — this is an identity):

DFBETASj(i) = sign(rij) · √(partial leverageij) · ti / √(1 − hi)
  • Partial leverage plij = rij² / Σk rkj² — how unusual the row's value of predictor j is after adjusting for all the other predictors (rj = residuals of regressing column j on the rest). This is the “which column” part: a row can be perfectly ordinary in every raw column and still have huge partial leverage on one of them, because its combination of values is unusual.
  • Studentized deleted residual ti — how badly the model misses this row's y. This part is shared by every coefficient, which is why a single wild y value lights up the whole row.

Both factors must be non-trivial to get a large DFBETAS. A row far out in x but perfectly on the line is harmless (t ≈ 0). A row with a wild residual sitting in the middle of the x-cloud pulls the intercept, not the slopes (pl ≈ 0 for the slopes). Cut-offs — and why not to trust them blindly
  • |DFBETAS| > 2/√n — the usual size-adjusted screening rule (Belsley, Kuh & Welsch). Designed to flag roughly a fixed small fraction of rows in large samples.
  • |DFBETAS| > 1 — the pragmatic rule for small n, where 2/√n is so low that it flags a third of the data.
  • Cut-offs are screening devices, not tests. In a clean dataset with n = 100 you should expect a handful of rows above 2/√n. What matters is the gap: one row an order of magnitude above the rest is a finding; twenty rows just over the line is normal noise.
How DFBETAS relates to leverage, Cook's D and DFFITS
  • Leverage hi — potential to be influential, from the x-values alone. y never enters. Rule of thumb: hi > 2p/n. High leverage is opportunity, not guilt.
  • Studentized deleted residual ti — is the row an outlier in y? Compare against a t-distribution with n − p − 1 df; |t| > 3 is worth a look.
  • Cook's D — one number per row for the influence on all fitted values at once. Great for triage, but it tells you nothing about which coefficient moved. Rules of thumb: D > 4/n (screening) or D > 1 (strict).
  • DFFITS — influence on that row's own fitted value; |DFFITS| > 2√(p/n).
  • DFBETAS — the only one that is per coefficient. Use Cook's D to find the suspects, DFBETAS to learn what they did.

Note the deletion diagnostics here are computed with the exact closed-form update (b − b(i) = (X′X)−1xiei / (1 − hi)), so results are identical to actually refitting n times — just faster.

You found an influential row. Now what?
  • Check it first. A large share of influential points are data errors: a decimal shifted, a unit mix-up, a missing-value sentinel like −999 or 9999 read as a real number.
  • Don't delete it just because it is influential. Deleting inconvenient rows biases both the estimates and every standard error you report. Influence is a property of the model as much as the row.
  • Ask what the row is telling you. A single point carrying the whole slope usually means the predictor's range is too thin to identify the effect, or that the functional form is wrong (a log or a quadratic often makes the point unremarkable).
  • Report both fits. “With and without observation 23” is a legitimate, honest robustness check — and far more informative than silently dropping it.
  • Consider robust regression (Huber, MM-estimators) if influential rows are genuine but you don't want them dominating.
  • Watch for masking. Two similar influential rows can hide each other: delete either one alone and nothing much happens. Single-row diagnostics cannot see this — if you suspect it, re-run after removing the top row and see what surfaces.
